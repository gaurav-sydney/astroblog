There's a version of the "agentic web" story that's all about the front end: an assistant that reads web pages,
clicks buttons and fills in forms on your behalf. It makes good demos. In practice, the first agents that get real
work done won't win by being better at clicking. They'll win because the organisation on the other side has said,
in a form a machine can rely on, what can be done and how.

Most of that work happens in the back office.

## What an agent needs from a website

Take a sports club, with a public website and a membership process behind it. A parent wants to register a child
for the winter season. Today that means reading three pages, downloading a form, paying through a separate link and
waiting for someone to reply to an email.

An agent acting for that parent needs four things the website rarely provides:

- **What can be done.** "Register a junior player for winter season" as a named action, not a paragraph of prose.
- **What it needs.** Date of birth, an emergency contact, the age group. Typed fields, not "please include".
- **What it costs, and the rules.** Fees, deadlines, the cases that need a committee decision.
- **What happens next.** A confirmation it can hand back: registered, waitlisted, or "a person will review this".

None of that is a design problem. It's a process problem that happens to surface on the website.

## The process is the product

Clubs and small organisations that automate well do the same thing first: they write the process down as steps,
each with one owner. The membership workflow becomes *receive the registration → check eligibility → take payment
→ add to the roster → confirm*. Some steps are software. Some are a volunteer with a spreadsheet. One or two are a
committee decision that should never be automated.

Once that's written down, three things become cheap:

1. **Automating the dull steps.** Payment, roster updates and confirmation emails run on their own.
2. **Showing people what's going on.** Every registration has a status someone can look up.
3. **Opening the door to agents.** The same steps, described with their inputs and outcomes, are exactly what an
   agent needs to act on someone's behalf.

> The agentic web isn't a new website. It's your existing process, written down clearly enough that software on
> either side can rely on it.

## Where to start

Don't start with an "AI strategy". Start with the one process people complain about most, usually bookings,
enrolments or memberships, and do three things:

- Write the steps down, with an owner for each, and mark the ones that need judgement.
- Automate one step end to end, including what happens when it fails.
- Publish the result as a clear action: a form whose fields match the steps, and a confirmation that says
  exactly what happened.

That's useful for people today. It's also, quietly, what makes your organisation reachable when the agents arrive.
